# Indian-Origin Professionals in US Quantum Computing Companies Without Green Cards

## Research Date: March 19, 2026

---

## Executive Summary

There is a **significant presence** of Indian-origin professionals in U.S. quantum computing companies, many of whom are likely operating without green cards due to the massive immigration backlog. Based on available data and best estimates:

- **Estimated 1,000–3,000+ Indian-origin professionals** work in U.S. quantum computing companies across engineering, research, and leadership roles
- The **EB-2 green card wait time for Indians is 12–15+ years** (with backlogs stretching up to 134 years in EB-3)
- Given that quantum computing is a relatively young industry (most companies founded 2015–2022), **a large majority of Indian-origin employees likely do NOT yet have green cards**
- Indian-origin professionals have risen to the **highest leadership levels**, including CEO of Rigetti Computing and CFO/COO of IonQ

## The Green Card Backlog Crisis

### Key Statistics
- **1.1 million Indian nationals** are in the employment-based green card backlog (out of 1.8 million total)
- **EB-2 India wait time**: Currently processing priority dates from July 2014 (as of April 2026 Visa Bulletin)
- **EB-3 India wait time**: Up to 134 years based on current processing rates
- **Annual cap**: 140,000 employment-based green cards total, with 7% per-country limit (~9,800 for India)

### Impact on Quantum Computing Workforce
- Most Indian quantum computing professionals who joined after 2012–2013 are **still waiting for green cards**
- Many are on **H-1B visas** (tied to employer), limiting their ability to change jobs or start companies
- Some founders use **O-1 visas** ("extraordinary ability") or **L-1 visas** (intracompany transfers)
- The **E-2 Treaty Investor visa is NOT available** to Indian nationals (India is not a treaty country)
- **National security implications**: Workers in quantum computing — a critical national security technology — face the same backlog as all other Indian STEM workers

### Common Visa Pathways for Indian Quantum Professionals

| Visa Type | Description | Availability for Indians |
|---|---|---|
| **H-1B** | Specialty occupation (employer-sponsored) | Yes, but lottery-based (85,000/year cap); $100,000 fee per application |
| **O-1** | Extraordinary ability | Yes, no cap; requires strong publication/achievement record |
| **L-1** | Intracompany transfer | Yes, for those with Indian operations |
| **OPT/STEM OPT** | Post-graduation work | Yes, 1–3 years for STEM graduates |
| **EB-1A** | Green card for extraordinary ability | Yes, shorter backlog (~2–4 years for India) |
| **EB-2 NIW** | National Interest Waiver | Yes, but heightened scrutiny in 2025–2026 |
| **EB-5** | Investor visa ($800K–$1.05M) | Yes, but requires substantial capital |
| **E-2** | Treaty investor | **NOT available** to Indian nationals |

## Key Observations

1. **Paradox of critical technology and immigration**: Quantum computing is classified as a critical and emerging technology by the U.S. government, yet Indian professionals working in this field face the same 134-year green card backlog as all other Indian workers.

2. **Brain drain risk**: As U.S. visa costs rise (H-1B fees increased to $100,000 per application) and wait times extend, Indian quantum talent is increasingly exploring opportunities in Canada, UK, Germany, Australia, and returning to India where the National Quantum Mission is investing $730M+.

3. **Startup barrier**: The lack of an E-2 treaty investor visa for Indian nationals makes it harder for Indian-origin entrepreneurs to found quantum startups in the U.S., pushing many toward the O-1 "genius visa" route.

4. **National security tension**: Many quantum computing roles at national labs and defense contractors require U.S. citizenship, creating a pipeline problem where highly skilled Indian quantum researchers cannot access the most sensitive work despite years of contribution.

5. **India's growing quantum ecosystem**: India now has 53+ active quantum computing companies, a $730M National Quantum Mission, and 251% funding growth in 2024–2025 — providing an increasingly attractive alternative for Indian quantum professionals frustrated with U.S. immigration barriers.
